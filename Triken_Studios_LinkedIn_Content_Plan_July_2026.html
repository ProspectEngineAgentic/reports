Post Drafts

Post 1: The Video Was Not the Problem

Image concept: Split visual. Left side shows a polished video frame sitting on a weak messy brief. Right side shows a clean Product Blueprint becoming the video. Clean white, navy, and cyan palette. No stock people.

The video was not the problem. The brief was. I have seen this happen many times. A team spends serious money on an explainer video. The animation looks good. The sound is clean. The motion is polished. But the buyer still does not get it. So the team asks for revisions. Then more revisions. Then someone from product says the script is wrong. Someone from sales says the customer will not understand it. Someone from marketing says the story feels flat. By then, the studio is only fixing symptoms. The real problem happened earlier. The product story was never mapped clearly before production started. Before a complex product becomes a video, the team needs to agree on five things. Who is this for? What do they misunderstand today? What does the product change? Why should they believe it? What should they remember after 60 seconds? If those answers are weak, animation cannot save the project. It can only make the confusion look more expensive.

Post 2: Your Buyer Is Not Inside Your Product Team

Image concept: Simple diagram with two circles. Product team language on one side. Buyer language on the other. Bridge labelled Product Story Map.

Your buyer is not inside your product team. That sounds obvious. But most product videos are still written like the buyer was in every internal meeting. The team explains the feature. The buyer needs the change. The team explains the mechanism. The buyer needs the risk removed. The team explains the platform. The buyer needs to know what life looks like after using it. This gap is where many explainer videos fail. Not because the product is weak. Not because the animation is bad. Because the story starts from the inside. A strong product story starts from the outside. What does the buyer already believe? Where are they stuck? What are they afraid to get wrong? What would make the product feel obvious? Once that is clear, the video has a job. Before that, it is just motion wrapped around internal language.

Post 3: Why We Built Product Story Mapping

Image concept: A clean canvas grid with nine labelled nodes. Narrative, audience, problem, mechanism, differentiator, outcome, proof, confusion points, visual direction.

Most discovery calls are too light. Most strategy projects are too slow. That is the gap we built Product Story Mapping for. Complex product teams do not need another casual call where everyone talks in circles. They need a working session that turns messy information into a clear explanation. Decks. Whitepapers. Website copy. Product notes. Sales objections. Internal opinions. All of it needs to become one clear blueprint. Not in three weeks. Not after five meetings. In one focused 90 minute session. The output is not a mood board. It is not a vague creative direction. It is a Product Blueprint. The story. The sequence. The buyer logic. The points of confusion. The scenes the video needs to carry. That is what should exist before production starts. Because once animation begins, unclear thinking gets expensive fast.

Post 4: AI Can Write Words. It Cannot Fix a Weak Product Story Alone

Image concept: Human creative director reviewing an AI structured canvas. Avoid robot imagery. Show the human judgment layer as the hero.

AI can write words. That does not mean it understands your product story. This is where many teams get disappointed. They paste product notes into a tool. They ask for a script. The script sounds clean. It even sounds confident. But it misses the part that matters. Why this product is hard to explain. Where the buyer gets lost. What the team is assuming. Which detail should be removed. Which detail must stay. Those are not writing problems. They are judgment problems. At Triken, we use AI inside a structured canvas because speed helps. But the canvas is built around creative direction logic. AI helps process the material. The system gives it structure. The human decides what matters. That combination is the point. Faster thinking is useful. Unstructured thinking, done faster, is still just noise.

Post 5: The Portfolio Request Is Usually Not the Real Question

Image concept: Inbox card reading "Can you send your portfolio?" with a deeper question underneath: "Can you solve our specific explanation problem?"

Can you send your portfolio? I understand why buyers ask this. They want proof. They want taste. They want to know if the studio can execute. But for complex products, the portfolio is only half the question. The better question is: Can you understand our product well enough to explain it? A beautiful healthcare video does not prove the next medtech product will be clear. A polished SaaS animation does not prove the buyer logic is right. A strong visual style does not fix a weak message. Execution matters. But explanation comes first. That is why I like to start with the product story. What is the buyer missing? What does the product make possible? What must be understood before the visual style even matters? The portfolio shows what a studio can make. The story map shows whether the next video is worth making.

Post 6: A Good Explainer Video Should Reduce Sales Effort

Image concept: Sales conversation before and after. Before: long call full of repeated explanation. After: buyer already understands the product before the meeting.

A good explainer video should reduce sales effort. Not replace sales. Reduce the repeated explanation. If your sales team still has to explain the same basic product idea from scratch on every call, the video is not doing its job. The buyer should arrive with a cleaner mental model. They should understand: What problem you solve. Why the old way is painful. How your product changes the process. Why the result matters. What question to ask next. That is when a video becomes useful. It moves the buyer from confusion to curiosity. It gives sales a better starting point. It turns the first call from education into diagnosis. That shift does not happen because the animation is smooth. It happens because the story was built around the buyer's confusion first.

Post 7: Your Product Is Not Too Complex. The Explanation Is Too Unstructured

Image concept: A tangled line becoming a clean sequence of five cards. Problem, shift, product, proof, outcome.

Your product is not too complex. The explanation is too unstructured. I hear this often from technical teams. Our product is hard to explain. Our buyer needs a lot of context. Our market is too specialised. Sometimes that is true. But often, the product is not the issue. The issue is that every team explains it from a different starting point. Product starts with features. Sales starts with objections. Marketing starts with the campaign. Leadership starts with the vision. All four may be right. But the buyer cannot hold four stories at once. They need one path. A clear beginning. A reason to care. A simple shift. A proof point. A next step. Complex products do not need to be dumbed down. They need to be sequenced.

Post 8: The Best Time To Fix The Video Is Before It Exists

Image concept: Timeline of a video project with a bright marker before script and storyboard labelled "story map".

The best time to fix the video is before it exists. Not after the first script. Not after the storyboard. Not after the animation preview. By then, every change is heavier. One unclear sentence becomes a script change. One missed buyer concern becomes a scene change. One internal disagreement becomes another revision round. This is why pre production matters so much for complex products. Not the checklist version. The thinking version. What are we really trying to explain? Who needs to understand it? What do they already believe? What will they resist? What proof will make the story land? Answer those questions early and the production moves faster. Skip them and the project still pays for them later. Usually with more meetings, more edits, and less patience.
