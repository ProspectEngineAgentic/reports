1The AI Act just gave credit scoring teams 16 extra months. Most will waste them.
News hook  EU Digital Omnibus deal, 7 May 2026: high risk AI rules (incl. credit scoring) move from Aug 2026 to 2 Dec 2027.

Last week the EU pushed the AI Act's high risk deadline back by 16 months.

If you score credit with AI, that is not relief. That is a runway.

Credit scoring is still high risk. The duties did not shrink. They just start on 2 December 2027 instead of August 2026. The deal was agreed on 7 May. It still needs final sign off.

So here is the trap. Most teams will read "delay" and move the work to the bottom of the list.

The hard part was never the model. The hard part is proving your training data was relevant, representative, and as clean as you could make it. And proving where every field came from.

That is data plumbing. It takes longer than a model swap. 16 months is not a lot when your loan tapes arrive every month from dozens of originators in different shapes.

My advice. Use the runway to fix the data layer now, while there is no fire.

Curious if your team sees the delay as relief or as runway.

6The biggest change in European loan data last year was not a new rule. It was a quiet switch off.
News hook  The ECB loan level reporting template was retired in Q4 2024; data now lands only in ESMA or FCA formats.

The biggest change in European loan data last year was not a flashy new rule. It was a quiet switch off.

In Q4 2024 the old ECB loan level reporting template was retired. Since then, securitisation loan data lands only in ESMA or FCA formats.

Sounds like a small admin change. It is not.

It means years of history now sit in one format, and everything new sits in another. If you want to study a portfolio across time, the two halves do not line up. The European DataWarehouse is even building a special All in One database just to stitch the old and new series back together.

This is the hidden tax of credit data. Formats change. Templates get revised. Your history fragments. And every team patches it by hand, again and again.

A clean data layer fixes this at the root. You map raw inputs to one standard shape, and you keep a record of every change. So when a template shifts, your time series survives.

This is exactly the kind of plumbing we build in the open.

7Banks moved 260 billion euros of risk off their books this way. Now regulators want a closer look.
News hook  Synthetic risk transfer (SRT) hit ~260bn euros in 2024 (record); BIS published an SRT risk paper in Q1 2026.

Banks moved about 260 billion euros of credit risk off their books in a single year using one tool. Now regulators want a closer look at it.

The tool is significant risk transfer, or SRT. A bank keeps the loans but sells off the risk on a slice of them, often to insurers and funds. It frees up capital. In 2024 it hit record volumes, and insurers nearly doubled how much they backed.

It got big enough that the BIS published a paper this year on the rise and risks of SRT, and the press started asking hard questions.

Here is the data angle most coverage misses.

Every SRT deal rests on a pool of loans. To price it, to monitor it, and to satisfy a regulator, you need clean, standard, loan level data on that pool. Buyers want to see what they are taking on. Supervisors want to see it too.

When the deals were rare, manual data work was fine. At record volume, hand cleaning every pool does not scale.

This is the quiet bottleneck behind a booming market. The data work has to grow with the deal flow.

Europe is rewriting its securitisation rulebook as we speak. And almost nobody is talking about what it does to the data work.

On 5 May 2026 the European Parliament passed its reform position. Now it goes to trilogue with the Council, likely landing around June 2027. The goal is to revive the market and cut some red tape.

One of the biggest fights is about private deals. The Council wants private securitisations to report through repositories. The Parliament wants a simpler template sent straight to national authorities.

Notice something. Both sides still want the data. They only disagree on the shape and the route.

This is the pattern of the last ten years. The rules change. The templates change. The duty to report clean loan data only grows.

The smart move is to stop building one off pipelines for each template. Build a clean base layer once, then map it to whatever shape the rule demands.

10Forty percent. That is how much of a loan pool can be marked "not applicable" in fields that decide price.
News hook  ESMA's template review shows large counts of "no data" codes in core fields; ESMA is simplifying private securitisation disclosure in 2025 to 2026.

Forty percent.

In a single loan pool, close to 40% of records can carry a "not applicable" code in fields that actually set the price. Things like the current interest rate margin.

Let that sink in. These are not side fields. They are core inputs for risk and pricing. And they arrive empty, hidden behind a tidy looking code.

This is not a fringe problem. ESMA has been reworking its disclosure templates, including a 2025 push to simplify private securitisation reporting, partly because the current data has so many of these gaps.

Here is the mistake teams make. They treat a "not applicable" code as if the value were truly zero or harmless. Then they train models and price deals on top of holes.

A model fed holes will sound confident and be wrong.

The honest fix is not to hide the gaps. It is to flag them, count them, and decide what to do about each one, on the record.

That is how we built our pipeline. It surfaces the gaps instead of papering over them. Because a gap you can see is a problem you can manage. A gap you cannot see is a loss waiting to happen.

If you model credit, how does your team handle the "not applicable" codes? I would genuinely like to know.
